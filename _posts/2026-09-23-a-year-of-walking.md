It was an absolute pleasure to close out a year of walking through internet infrastructure in London by returning where it all started at Open City's [Open House Festival](https://programme.openhouse.org.uk).  I thought I'd write a bit about how these walks started, as they've shape-shifted throughout the year.
  
I first gave this walk at the festival last year, after the encouragement of two friends, Brandon Jackson and Justin Manley – both of which are graduates of Open City's "[Golden Key Academy](https://open-city.org.uk/golden-key-academy)" . Little did I know that this would become a year-long experiment!

The original question behind the tour was: "How can I show people how to spot internet infrastructure around them?". I was inspired by the work (and walks!) of [Ingrid Burrington](https://lifewinning.com), [Mario Santamaría](https://www.mariosantamaria.net), and the [critical infrastructure lab](https://www.criticalinfralab.net), who took their critical approaches to technology and brought them closer to the street, teaching walkers how to identify the infrastructure behind surveillance, connectivity, and our data-driven lives. In 2024 at the Mozilla Festival in Amsterdam, I participated in a data center walk by [Yan Cong](https://www.yan-cong.com). I remembered Ingrid's walks around a decade ago in New York City, and when I moved to London back in 2022, I'd thought that it could be interesting or even valuable to organise something like that in London. Around the same time, I was conducting research with the [Internet Society on low earth orbit satellites](https://vimeo.com/722329842), an emergent form of connectivity at the time (check out my 'above the clouds and below the sea' project for more about this). All of these projects and inspirations culminated in the desire to organise a similar kind of walk with a similar kind of criticality.

People responded in such interesting ways to that first walk at the OHF! Folks joined from all around my personal and professional lives joined, as well as complete strangers that were just interested in the topic. Folks from ISOC joined, from the Turing, from are.na and beyond, and to be honest, I was totally blown away by the response. They had all sorts of questions: Why is internet so bad on the tube? Why are we in the City of London specifically? I've heard that the internet is better in Shoreditch - why is this the case? Is this true?

After those two walks, months passed. But they were such a rewarding experience that I ultimately organised a follow-up for the Open Knowledge Foundation's [Open Data Days](https://opendataday.org) the following March. Over these two weeks, even more folks joined the ISOC network, ex-BT engineers and university professors, and people who had somehow heard about the walk through word of mouth somehow. I made a page on Luma to organise sign-ups. 

As a bit of a challenge to myself (and honestly, also because I felt a bit stuck due to ), I decided to organise a walk every month for internet-related holidays over the summer, from April to August. 

As the walk evolved throughout the year, the questions that the walk was trying to answer . story belying the walk eventually became: "What is a story worth telling about the internet in the UK?" or rather, "What is a story about the internet that is unique to the UK?" 

This What emerged what that set of questions is rather different, woven in and around how connectivity was imagined and constructed here. In many ways, the walk became a retrospective of the history of telecommunications in the UK, and the contours of power and capital that ultimately shaped the internet itself. 

A year later, I've given walks for [****Open Knowledge Foundation****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/okfn/)'s Open Data Days, [****London Data Week****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/london-data-week/), [****Projects by IF****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/projects-by-if/), [****University of the Arts London****](https://www.linkedin.com/school/university-of-the-arts-london/) and [****London School of Solarpunk****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/londonschoolofsolarpunk/) and others, and a handful around a number of internet and tech-related "holidays".  
There are so many things I've learned from these walks. The first is that these walks have been like a pulse on how people think about technology in a way that no other project that I've worked on so far has. People have joined from all walks of life (so to speak): civil servants from councils, technologists from all sorts of companies, artists, researchers, engineers, infrastructure enthusiasts, teachers, journalists, and everyday folks interested in learning more about technology.
  
Walkers have also become collaborators and friends, and some have even started giving walks themselves (spot: [****Michael Kibedi****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/uxmichaelco/) at [****Serpentine****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/serpentine-galleries/) a few weeks ago!). Each one was different, and brought another angle, question or research rabbit hole.  


Perhaps my biggest takeaway is how out of step the pace of technology and policy can be with how people actually understand something as ubiquitous as the internet. Amidst all the doomsday scenarios about AI and data centres, we still have a long way to go in explaining that the internet is more than “the cloud” – let alone figuring out how to regulate it.  
  
In any case, it's been a joy. Thank you for joining, for walking, and for listening.
