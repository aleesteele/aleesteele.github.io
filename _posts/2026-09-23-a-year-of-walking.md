It was wonderful  to close out a year of walking through internet infrastructure in London by returning where it all started at Open City's [Open House Festival](https://programme.openhouse.org.uk).  I thought I'd write a bit about how these walks started, as they've shape-shifted throughout the year.
  
I first gave this walk at the festival last year, after the encouragement of two friends, [Brandon Jackson](http://brandonjackson.org) and [Justin Manley](https://justinmanley.world/about/) – both of which are graduates of Open City's [Golden Key Academy](https://open-city.org.uk/golden-key-academy). I'd first told Brandon about this idea at a meet-up for [Public AI](https://publicai.network), and like all signs of a good new friend, he said: "What are you doing two weekends from now? Show me a prototype of the walk, and I'll bring a friend to help give feedback".

Little did I know that this would become a year-long experiment.

The original question behind the tour was: "How can I show people how to spot internet infrastructure around them?". I was inspired by the work (and walks!) of [Ingrid Burrington](https://lifewinning.com), [Mario Santamaría](https://www.mariosantamaria.net), and the [critical infrastructure lab](https://www.criticalinfralab.net), who seemed to take their critical approaches to technology and bring them closer to the street, teaching walkers how to identify the infrastructure behind surveillance, connectivity, and our data-driven lives. I'd actually participated in a walk back in 2024 at the Mozilla Festival in Amsterdam, a data center walk led by [Yan Cong](https://www.yan-cong.com) that brought us to two two nondescript buildings, one of which we were able to enter, the other of which remained observable only from the outside. Around the same time, I was conducting research with the [Internet Society on low earth orbit satellites](https://vimeo.com/722329842), an emergent form of connectivity at the time. All of these projects and inspirations culminated in the desire to organise a similar kind of walk with a similar kind of criticality. I'd been based in London since 2022, but I hadn't seen anything similar within the city.

The original two walks sold out in less than 5 minutes, which totally shocked me. That year, the main website got so many visitors that in crashed. Turns out, the Open House Festival is a popular event, and people were more interested in internet infrastructure than I thought they would be. 

People responded in such interesting ways to that first walk at the festival. Friends and associates joined from all across my personal and professional networks, alongside complete strangers that were just interested in the topic. Folks from ISOC joined, from the Turing, from are.na and beyond. To be honest, I was totally blown away by the response. People also had all sorts of questions: Why is internet so bad on the tube? Why are we in the City of London specifically? I've heard that the internet is better in Shoreditch - why is this the case? What cable is my neighborhood connected to?

After those two walks, months passed. I was interested in organising them again, but I wasn't quite sure where or when would be a good time. 

I ended up organising a follow-up pair for the Open Knowledge Foundation's [Open Data Days](https://opendataday.org) the following March, an event I'd participated in a few years before as a Fellow. Over these two weeks, even more folks joined from the ISOC network, ex-BT engineers and university professors, and people who had somehow heard about the walk through word of mouth. I made a page on Luma to organise sign-ups. Again, I was totally shocked by the interest people had in the subject - and by the questions and personal anecdotes of people working in the industry.

Over the next few months, I decided to organise a walk every month for internet-related holidays or festivals over the summer, from April to August. By "holidays", I meant everything from National Surveillance Camera Day to Earth Day, from International Internet Day to London Data Week and everything in-between.

As the walk evolved throughout the year, the questions that the walk was trying to answer evolved, and so did the route in turn. The question behind it became: "What is a story worth telling about the internet in the UK?" or rather, "**What is a story about the internet that is unique to the UK?**" 

This question emerged because in Britain, internet connectivity is intrinsically connected to the industries that have risen within it (particularly finance in the City) as well as the histories of empire that far preceded their modern counterparts. To talk about the internet in the UK requires talking about the broader history of telecommunications, and telling a story that traces the contours of that history into the present. In many ways, the walk became a retrospective of the history of telecommunications in the UK, and the power, capital, and colonial processes that ultimately shaped the internet itself. 

As these walks evolved with the audience that attended them, I soon realised that they're kind of like a pulse on how people think about technology – in a way that no other project that I've worked on so far has been. People have joined from all walks of life (so to speak): civil servants from local councils, technologists from all sorts of companies, artists, researchers, engineers, infrastructure enthusiasts, teachers, journalists, and everyday folks interested in learning more about technology.

Walkers have also become collaborators and friends, and some have even started giving walks themselves (spot: [****Michael Kibedi****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/uxmichaelco/) at [****Serpentine****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/serpentine-galleries/) a few weeks ago!). Each one was different, and brought another angle, question or research rabbit hole.  

Perhaps my biggest takeaway is how out of step the pace of technology and policy can be with how people actually understand something as ubiquitous as the internet. Amidst all the doomsday scenarios about AI and data centres, we still have a long way to go in explaining that the internet is more than “the cloud” – let alone figuring out how to regulate it.  
  
In any case, it's been a joy. Thank you for joining, for walking, and for listening.
